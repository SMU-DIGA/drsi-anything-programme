DRSI-Anything

Delegative Recursive Self-Improvement across General Domains

SMU-DIGA

Design Environments Results Progress Roadmap BibTeX GitHub

Deep RL updates weights. Prompt optimization tunes instructions. We let LLMs rewrite the program itself, keep a version only if it wins on seeds it never saw, and repeat.

TL;DR

An LLM writes a program that solves a task, such as an Atari bot, a science-experiment agent or a desktop agent. A population of LLM improvers (Codex CLI, Claude Code, DeepSeek) then improves that program over many generations, using only feedback from interaction with the environment. Promotion is gated on hidden seeds, and we evaluate on game, text science-experiment, ARC-AGI and OSWorld benchmarks the community reports on most often.

Abstract

Deep reinforcement learning improves agents by adjusting weights. Recent evidence suggests that coding agents editing hand-written programs can reach competitive scores on Atari, MuJoCo and VizDoom without any gradient step, and that cross-domain recursive self-improvement loops benefit from frozen evaluation protocols. DRSI-Anything combines the two into a delegative loop: the models that reason and edit (LLMs) do not act in the environment themselves; they delegate the acting to an artifact they write and keep improving, and what is learned is stored in that delegate. Our main delegate is a neuro-symbolic one, readable, testable code and notes that persist across generations, optionally calling smaller models as tools, whose weights an improver may also write directly (weight-space programming, planned); neuro-symbolic programs are one way to delegate, not the only one. A program, consisting of a policy, its skills and its notes, is improved generation after generation by a heterogeneous population of LLM improvers. Each candidate is re-evaluated in an isolated process and promoted only by a paired test on hidden seeds. We study when such loops keep improving, what transfers across tasks, and how they compare with RL under the same environment-step budget, on games (Atari), text science experiments (ScienceWorld), abstract reasoning (ARC-AGI-1, 2 and 3) and OSWorld.

Design

LLMs improve a program; a version is kept only if it wins on seeds no one saw.

1

Propose

Each improver edits its own copy of the current program, with feedback and an evaluator on train seeds only.

2

Re-evaluate

Every candidate is re-scored in isolation; scores the improver reports are ignored.

3

Promote

The best candidate replaces the incumbent only if a paired bootstrap test on hidden dev seeds says it is better.

4

Simplify

Optionally, the winner is shortened, and the shorter version is kept only if its dev score does not drop.

Robustness can be improved too

With sticky actions in the loop, the improvers made the same programs robust. Ms. Pac-Man went from 10,029 to 38,306 on sticky test seeds, two thirds of its no-sticky test score. Montezuma's Revenge went from 3,441 to 113,659 on dev, above its no-sticky score, with real spread across seeds (28,200 to 65,300 for the generation 6 champion), so it is not one memorised game. Under sticky actions dev seeds also stopped tying: all 32 Ms. Pac-Man dev seeds score differently, which makes the paired gate a real test of generalization.

Sticky actions: what survives

Every score drops, so part of each no-sticky result came from a deterministic game whose play can be memorised. How much drops follows how the program works. Pong, Breakout and Space Invaders re-plan from the live state at every step and keep half or more. Ms. Pac-Man keeps 18%: it relies on exact timing, such as passing a junction just ahead of a ghost. Montezuma's Revenge keeps 5%: its route is scripted against countdowns and distances, and one mistimed jump costs a life. Under sticky actions four of the five champions still score above the human reference (Breakout about 15×, Space Invaders about 12×, Ms. Pac-Man 1.6×, Pong slightly); Montezuma's Revenge does not. On the same fresh seeds without sticky actions Ms. Pac-Man scored 61,084, above its dev score, so dev itself was not overfitted; the weakness is determinism.

The gate caught overfitting

In generation 3 a candidate scored the maximum on all 8 train seeds but only 714 on dev: it had tuned itself to the few train trajectories. It was rejected. Generation 4 reached 864 on every dev seed and then on every one of the 64 test seeds, which no improver or gate had ever touched.

Recursive improvement, nine generations in a row

Ms. Pac-Man was promoted in every generation so far: 17,207, 19,173, 20,031, 25,663, 29,688, 30,715, 32,295, 34,174, then 50,545 on hidden dev, each version built on the previous one and each winning the paired dev test. The first program decoded the maze, dots and ghosts from RAM and planned over a maze graph. Later generations added escapes at junctions when a ghost is close, detours for fruit, more cautious routing after quick life losses and, in generation 9, a reflex that reverses when a ghost comes head-on on the last life.

A caveat on what dev measures here. Without sticky actions the game is nearly deterministic, so many dev seeds replay the same game. Generation 9 tied its parent exactly on 25 of 32 seeds and won on 7: on those 7 it no longer dies at the same spot and plays to the 27,000-step limit (37,491 → about 112,330). The gain is real, but it fixes one failure that recurs across seeds rather than showing broad generalization; Table 4 shows the cost under sticky actions.

Montezuma's Revenge without exploration bonuses

The promoted program follows an explicit room-by-room route over RAM state (room, position, inventory, enemy positions, and a laser countdown it discovered). It times jumps on the skull's live position and laser crossings on the countdown, collects keys, treasures and a sword, and opens four doors. Where a respawn keeps the inventory, it loses a life on purpose as a shortcut back. Generation 6 extended the route through level 2, keeping both keys for the exit doors and collecting its prizes, and into level 3 (53,700 on every dev seed). Caveat: without sticky actions, different seeds mostly shift start-up timing, so dev mainly tests robustness to timing; under sticky actions it keeps only 5% (Table 4); the sticky run brought it back (Table 2).

Space Invaders by aiming, not by luck

The program located the invader grid, its speed and descent, both enemy bullets and the shields in RAM, and fitted the missile's flight time from about 1,700 kills. It fires with a lead computed from the grid's motion and steps out of the path of falling bullets.

For scale: the readable Leduc policy (test 0.106) sits between 30 and 100 iterations of vanilla CFR run on each test variant, which reaches 0.005 after 1,000 iterations. It generalizes across variants without solving any of them, but it is still far from an equilibrium.

HUNL: the champion beats the rule bots (call station, maniac, equity bot) and earlier champions by 29,090 mbb/hand on 64 test seeds, but those opponents are easy to exploit. Against DecisionHoldem, a strong published agent, the same program loses about 4.6 big blinds per hand (± 5.7, standard deviation over 8 seeds of 50 hands each), so beating weak opponents did not make it strong. The starting program (always call) scores −1.7 big blinds per hand (± 4.2) on the same 8 seeds, so against DecisionHoldem the champion is lower than its starting point (shown in red); with 8 seeds and a standard deviation this large the gap is within noise. DecisionHoldem's search makes every hand take seconds, so this is a small sample; a loop that trains against DecisionHoldem directly is being set up.

A run we threw away

The first HUNL run reported +62,790 mbb/hand, which was too good to be true. Its program had found a hole in our harness: the mirrored hand of each duplicate pair was replayed right away in the same process, so it could remember the board and its own earlier cards, which were now its opponent's. Each seat is now played by a separate, fresh process; every deal is seen once per process; and temp files, sockets, subprocesses and native code are rejected. The run was restarted from scratch.

How to read these numbers

No demonstrations and no learned weights: every policy is readable code. State mode is easier than the VLA setting and is not comparable to it. Vision mode is the closest match, but it uses depth and camera calibration, which the VLA models do not. Programs contain routines per task family and generalize across unseen initial scenes, which is what LIBERO's protocol tests.

* LIBERO-10 vision ran before the seed guard existed. One improver (generation 5) evaluated its candidate on 40 dev scenes through a hand-edited task file, and the generation-6 improver whose program became the champion read that candidate's notes and code, so the dev number is optimistic. Test scenes were never touched; 87.0% is a clean test estimate (per task 65–100%; the microwave task is the weakest). The guard now refuses any seed outside train.

What the programs found by probing

The vision program measured that its agent-view estimate of a bowl's centre is biased by about 4 cm, so it re-centres with the wrist camera before grasping. The LIBERO-10 program worked out each goal condition (e.g. the stove counts as on once its knob turns past about 0.45 rad) and found initial scenes where objects fly off the table on the first step, which no policy can solve.

LIBERO-Spatial vision finished its 10 generations at 95.0% on dev. In the last generation two candidates reached 96.3%, but one extra win was not enough for the paired gate. On the 400 test episodes it scored 95.3%, in line with dev. Running next: the vision setting on LIBERO-10, LIBERO-Object and LIBERO-Goal, each starting from the LIBERO-Spatial vision champion, so that all four suites compare with OpenVLA-OFT. ManiSkill3 is queued.

A large train–dev gap

Both champions solve most train tasks (65% and 78%) but few dev tasks. They are skill libraries: regular expressions map an instruction to a handler that scripts LibreOffice through its UNO interface or runs shell and settings commands. A handler written for a train task rarely covers a dev task, which usually needs a different skill. The better run (5 / 24) probes the desktop first and has 28 spreadsheet and about 20 desktop handlers.

The model was available but not used

The 5 / 24 champion never calls the small model; only unpromoted candidates did. So the difference between the two runs is two independent searches over pure-code programs, not evidence that the model helps. The next runs cover eight applications (Calc, Writer, Impress, OS, VS Code, GIMP, VLC, Thunderbird; 222 tasks split by application into 70 / 76 / 76) and tell the improvers where the model can help, so the comparison becomes a real ablation.

The program decides every action in code, including running code inside the desktop and reading its output. The model never writes text or actions; it returns a probability per option and acts as a smart if-statement inside the program (in the style of TypeSafe's Jev). In both settings the program may learn a memory from its own train episodes, frozen during dev and test.

ScienceWorld: one program for 30 science experiments

ScienceWorld (Wang et al., 2022) is a text world of a house with a kitchen, greenhouse, workshop and so on: the agent boils and melts substances, wires circuits, tests conductivity, grows plants, reads life stages, times blocks on inclined planes and crosses plants for Mendelian traits. Each of its 30 task types has variations (other substances, objects, plants, animals, rooms) that ScienceWorld itself splits into train, dev and test. We evolve a single program for all 30 types. It reads the text observation, room, inventory, task score and the list of valid commands at every step and answers with one command, so it has to react to what it sees: the champion runs experiments (it closes a circuit and checks whether the bulb lights, heats a substance while reading a thermometer) rather than replaying a script. Seeds: 60 train (2 per type), 90 hidden dev (3 per type), test as below; ScienceWorld's easy simplification, at most 100 commands per episode. The program calls no model, cannot import ScienceWorld, and never sees the gold action sequence.

Round 2 starts from the round-1 champion (generation 10) with more seeds (300 train, 150 other hidden dev) and a new instruction: first classify the causes of failure in the transcripts of failing episodes, then fix the largest cause with one closed-loop mechanism (act, check the effect, recover). Improvers: Claude Code and DeepSeek (Codex only in its first generation). It was promoted in all 6 generations and stopped by hand during generation 7, when train had saturated (99.3%). On all 1,819 test variations it raised the mean from 59.3 to 67.5, and on SwiftSage's first 10 variations per type from 62.5 to 71.3 (SwiftSage 84.68).

Round 3 starts from the round-2 champion with 600 train and 300 other hidden dev seeds, a stricter gate (confidence 0.9) and Claude Code only (one Opus, two Sonnet), each improver assigned a group of ScienceWorld topics. The instruction asks first for a generalization audit: remove every branch keyed on an object seen in train, and share one skill per capability across task types. After generation 3 we analysed the test failures of the change-of-state types and added one general rule (a default that only works on train is a special case; search the whole world for the exact target and never substitute another object; escalate to another means when one has no effect); it names no test object, but it was written after looking at test, which we report here. From generation 5 the train seeds change every generation, every candidate within 0.5 points of the incumbent's train goes to the dev gate (the best passing one is promoted), and there is no simplify pass. So far: 67.5 → 69.8 on all test variations after 3 generations (genetics of an unknown plant 37.5 → 77.5, friction 77.6 → 100), then → 78.1 → 80.8 after generations 5 and 6 (dev 89.1 → 90.8 → 92.9, both promotions from the Opus improver on change of state): boil 0.7 → 75.9, melt 0.4 → 100, freeze 0 → 58.4, change state 0.2 → 93.1. On SwiftSage's first 10 variations per type this is 82.1 (SwiftSage 84.68); the largest remaining gaps are power component (8.2 vs 88.7), renewable energy (6.0 vs 55.3), melting point of a known substance (19.5 vs 88.2) and life stages 2 (25.0 vs 68.5). Generation 7 promoted nothing: train was at 99% and every one of these types scored 1.0 on train, so the improvers saw no failure to fix (power component has only 10 official train variations). We stopped round 3 during generation 8.

Round 4 starts from g006c0 and splits ScienceWorld's official dev variations into two disjoint halves: one joins train (300 of each generation's 750 train seeds), the other stays the hidden promotion gate; test is unchanged. On the half moved to train the incumbent scores 0.07 on power component, so the failures are now visible to the improvers without looking at test. Generation 1 promoted g001c1 (dev 92.1 → 94.4; freeze 58.4 → 94.9 on test): 82.7 on all test variations, 84.25 on SwiftSage's protocol.

Composition. Next to round 4 we ran three specialists from g001c1, each a short DRSI run restricted to a few task types (electricity; heat; life stages, chemistry, genetics and lifespans), with two Sonnet improvers asked to build every fix as a self-contained, general skill so that it can be merged back. The electricity specialist found the cause of the power-component failures in its first generation: the circuit skill only handled tasks that light a bulb, so "turn on the electric motor" (and any other target) fell through; it now reads the target from the task text and searches the world for it (specialist dev 87.3 → 100). Its program is g001c1 with only the circuit skill changed, so we gated it like any candidate, on all 30 types of round 4's hidden dev: 94.4 → 96.2 (7 wins, 1 loss, P(gain) 0.999). On test: power component 8.2 → 46.4, renewable energy 6.0 → 34.0, no type lower by more than 2 points; 85.0 on all 1,819 test variations and 86.8 on SwiftSage's protocol, above SwiftSage's 84.68 (GPT-4 at run time; ours uses no model at run time). In round 4 we ran the full test after every promotion and stopped all runs once the SwiftSage-protocol score passed 84.68; test was only this stopping rule, never shown to an improver or used to pick a candidate. We then merged the heat and life-science specialists too, by a plain three-way text merge (all three started from g001c1 and changed different skills; no conflict). The merged program scores 96.5 on round 4's dev (2 wins, 2 losses: not a significant gain, so our gate would not promote it) but 86.4 on all test variations and 88.5 on SwiftSage's protocol (melting point of a known substance 27.8 → 65.4, genetics of an unknown plant 76.1 → 82.5): that skill is already at 100 on dev, so dev cannot see the gain. Because it did not pass the gate, our headline stays 86.8; we report 88.5 here as the merge we did not select.

ARC-AGI-1, 2 and 3: one program

The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus asks for a rule inferred from a few examples. We put all three versions into one task family solved by one program: ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2 are static puzzles (a few input–output grid pairs, then a test input; two attempts, exact match, as in ARC's pass@2), and ARC-AGI-3 is a set of interactive turn-based games with several levels and no instructions (64 × 64 frames, keyboard-like actions and clicks, played offline with the official engine, score weighted by level as in ARC-AGI-3). The program reads obs["kind"] and must handle both; it is never told the puzzle's or game's name, nor whether an answer was right.

ARC-AGI-1 rises; ARC-AGI-2 and ARC-AGI-3 do not yet

The program is a generate-and-verify solver: it searches grid transformations (symmetries and colour maps, scaling and tiling, symmetry and period completion, boolean operations on split parts, object selection and movement, local rule tables, two-step compositions) and answers only with one that reproduces every demonstration exactly. On unseen ARC-AGI-1 puzzles it climbs from 30.5% to 40.0% in six generations. On ARC-AGI-2 it solves 1 of 80 dev puzzles throughout: its train score (29–35%) comes from revised ARC-AGI-1 puzzles inside ARC-AGI-2 training, and dev uses only puzzles new in ARC-AGI-2. Train also overfits: in generation 3 an Opus candidate grew to 4,189 lines and reached 68.5% on its ARC-AGI-1 train puzzles but only 36% on dev, short of the gate. In the games, an early candidate cleared the first level of three dev games, but no promoted version clears any.

Paused after generation 6 (of at most 10); six generations cost $212 in improver sessions; test not yet run. Three Claude improvers (Opus and two Sonnet); the improvers cannot read the data, the split manifest or the game engine, and the guard rejects a program that does.

Most environment steps are spent by the improvers' own trial runs, not by the gates. An Atari-5 run uses 18–76 M steps (71–303 M frames): the same order as the standard deep-RL budget of 200 M frames, and far above sample-efficient settings such as Atari 100k (400 K frames). Sample efficiency is a weakness of this loop. In money a run costs tens of dollars of agent time.
